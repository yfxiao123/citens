# 订单簿建模

## 引言 / Introduction

The limit order book (LOB) is the foundational mechanism of modern electronic financial markets. It records, in real time, all outstanding buy and sell limit orders at discrete price levels, together with their sizes, times, and order types. Every marketable order, every cancellation, and every revision updates this state, and the resulting sequence of order arrivals and removals ultimately determines the best bid and ask, the depth of liquidity, and the micro-level trajectory of transaction prices. In the past two decades, the migration of virtually all major asset classes to electronic limit order platforms has made the LOB not merely a microstructure curiosity but the operational core of price formation. Consequently, the ability to model the order book with mathematical and statistical fidelity has become essential for a wide range of activities: high-frequency trading, optimal execution, market making, transaction cost analysis, market risk measurement, and, increasingly, the regulatory oversight of market quality and systemic risk.

The scientific interest in LOB modeling is driven by an unusually rich set of empirical features. Order arrivals are bursty and strongly autocorrelated; large market orders tend to induce temporary price shifts; liquidity at nearby price levels is replenished in a way that exhibits both fast decay and long-range dependence; and the entire book evolves under a mixture of exogenous information flow and endogenous reaction to its own dynamics. Any credible model must accommodate the discreteness of prices and sizes, the asynchronicity of event times, the mutual excitation between buy- and sell-side activity, and the sheer dimensionality of a state that includes dozens of price levels with conditional dependencies across them. Moreover, the LOB is not stationary. It alternates between calm and turbulent regimes, reacts asymmetrically to good and bad news, and its dynamics differ across assets, across market structures, and across time of day. These features render classical continuous-price models inadequate and explain the emergence of a vibrant interdisciplinary literature drawing on market microstructure theory, applied probability, econometrics, queuing theory, and, most recently, deep learning.

Despite this progress, fundamental open problems remain. First, no consensus exists on the appropriate level of abstraction. Some models treat the LOB as a Markovian queueing system with only a few levels; others attempt full state-space descriptions involving hundreds of state variables. The former are analytically tractable but often too stylized to reproduce high-frequency price dynamics; the latter capture empirical richness but are difficult to estimate and hard to analyze theoretically. Second, the interplay between event time and physical time remains only partially understood. Order flow is naturally generated by human and algorithmic agents acting at irregular intervals, and any model that assumes equally spaced observations throws away information embedded in durations. Third, while Hawkes processes have become a standard tool for modeling the self- and cross-excitation of order arrivals, they are primarily univariate or low-dimensional in practice, whereas the full LOB contains strong dependencies across many price levels. Fourth, machine learning models, particularly deep neural networks, have achieved impressive predictive performance on order-flow and mid-price movement tasks, but they often operate as black boxes, offering little insight into the structural mechanisms of price formation and lacking robust performance guarantees outside the training distribution. Finally, there is a persistent gap between theoretical models of the LOB and their practical calibration to real data, compounded by the sheer size and event-nested structure of high-frequency data sets.

In view of these challenges, this review aims to provide a systematic and accessible synthesis of the current state of order book modeling. Rather than emphasizing a single methodological camp, this review pursues a comparative perspective, placing econometric, stochastic, queueing, and learning-based approaches in a unified framework. The structure is as follows. Section 2 introduces the key notation, data structures, and stylized facts of the LOB that any model must reproduce. Section 3 reviews econometric and stochastic models, including autoregressive conditional duration models, state-space formulations, and Markov chain approaches. Section 4 treats queueing-theoretic models, with particular attention to zero-intelligence models and their extensions. Section 5 focuses on Hawkes processes and related point-process models of order arrival dynamics. Section 6 discusses the recent wave of machine learning and deep learning methods, including neural network architectures for LOB prediction and generative models for LOB simulation. Section 7 highlights applications in optimal execution, market making, and market impact assessment. Section 8 concludes by outlining outstanding methodological challenges and promising directions for future research. Through this structure, this review hopes to serve both as a gateway for newcomers and as a thoughtful roadmap for specialists seeking to identify the most pressing open problems in this fast-moving field.

### 订单簿模型与市场微观结构

The three papers gathered under this theme converge on a shared premise: the limit order book is not merely a passive record of submitted orders but a generative structure from which price dynamics, trading costs, and market efficiency can be derived. Yet they approach this premise from different microeconomic and statistical directions, and their differences reveal where the research frontier currently struggles. [6] constructs the order book from first principles within an information-asymmetry framework, extending the canonical Glosten-Milgrom setup to infer the full bid-ask spread and the volume available at each price. [4] takes the opposite, more phenomenological route, asking what the order book’s information content implies for stochastic price dynamics and the emergence of fat tails. [5] shifts the setting to matching markets, where monetary transfers may not be sufficient to guarantee market clearing, and analyzes how order-flow dynamics, spread formation, and execution slippage interact under such structural frictions.

Read together, these papers map out a clear intellectual lineage and a complementarity of method. [6] supplies the microfoundational base: by modeling informed traders, noise traders, and competitive market makers, it shows that observable features of the whole order book—spread, depth, price-dependent volume—can be interpreted as equilibrium outcomes of adverse selection and competitive quoting. This is a generative account: the order book is an implication of asymmetric information and market-making constraints, and the framework is explicitly oriented toward financial regulation. [4] moves from structure to implication. Rather than asking why the book has the shape it does, it asks what that shape tells us: the order book contains information sufficient to determine the heavy-tailed distribution of price changes, so the mechanics of limit order placement and cancellation are directly linked to the stylized facts of returns. In this sense, [4] is the natural downstream counterpart of [6]—if the order book is a well-defined object, then its informational content can be inverted into stochastic price dynamics. [5] then complicates the picture by questioning the medium through which market clearing occurs. In conventional order book models, including [6], prices and monetary transfers are the natural equilibrating variables; in matching markets, however, structural preference gaps can persist even when transfers are available, so spread and execution slippage become consequential in ways that financial-market models may not capture. The three papers thus form a triangle: [6] explains where the order book comes from, [4] explains what the order book predicts, and [5] explains when those predictions may fail because non-price constraints bind.

The most instructive tension among the papers concerns the informational sufficiency of the order book. [4] asserts that the limit order book contains enough information to determine fat tails in financial data, effectively claiming that the observable order book is sufficient for the distributional properties of prices. [5] does not directly contradict this claim, but its central finding—that monetary transfers alone cannot clear matched markets when preference gaps are structural—implies that an order book model built solely on price-clearing channels will miss an important class of frictions. This is not an irreconcilable conflict so much as a difference in domain and scope. [4] is concerned with financial price distributions, where a well-developed institutional structure of dealers, market makers, and monetary exchange makes price-based clearing plausible. [5] is concerned with the broader class of matching markets, where a shortage of acceptable monetary compensation means the book, the spread, and slippage carry non-price allocative content. The apparent contradiction, then, is better read as a boundary condition: order book information is sufficient for prices only in environments where prices are the sufficient clearing device. This reading is consonant with [6], whose competitive market-maker assumption is precisely a statement that the quoting side of the market can adjust prices until expected profits are zero; [5] reminds us that such an assumption is not neutral and may fail when preference heterogeneity cannot be monetized.

A second, subtler divergence runs between [6] and [4]. [6] derives the whole order book from the behavior of informed and uninformed traders, implying that order book shape is an outcome of information processing. [4] instead treats the order book as a data-rich object and derives price dynamics directly from its information content. The former is structural and causal; the latter is informational and statistical. They are not in contradiction, but they embody different commitments about where the explanatory burden lies. [6] makes the book endogenous to an information structure, which is attractive for regulatory applications because it permits counterfactual analysis of rule changes. [4] makes prices endogenous to the book, which is attractive for empirical work because it connects the book to measurable return distributions. A future synthesis would need to articulate how the information structure that generates the book in [6] is encoded in, and recoverable from, the book’s empirical information that [4] exploits.

Where does this leave the theme? The papers collectively argue that order book modeling is not a single task but a family of tasks—derivation, inference, and robustness analysis—each with distinct assumptions and failures. My own position is that the tension between [4] and [5] is the most productive one for future research. It tells us that “information contained in the limit order book” is not invariant: its sufficiency depends on the market’s institutional capacity to clear through money, and once that capacity breaks down, the book’s role as a sufficient statistic for price dynamics weakens. The regulatory ambition of [6] would therefore be well advised to incorporate the structural-preference channels identified by [5]. Such a merger would not abandon the insight that the order book condenses market information, but it would insist that the information is conditional on the market’s matching environment. This is a more demanding, and more realistic, basis for microstructure theory.

### 最优执行与市场影响

The three papers in this theme converge on a single question: how should a trader size and time orders when execution itself changes the limit order book? Their shared premise is that market impact is not an exogenous cost but a consequence of how orders deplete and restore book liquidity. Starting from that premise, they move together from a micro-structural benchmark to constrained optimization and finally to a general characterization of trading strategies that are free from price manipulation. The line of development is one of increasing generality, but it is also a sequence of trade-offs between realism and tractability.

The consensus across the three studies is strong. All three treat the limit order book as the fundamental object from which execution costs and optimal policies should be derived. [3] constructs a market impact model endogenously from order book dynamics, with the impact function depending on order size and liquidity. [1] extends a limit order book framework to describe how market orders affect liquidity and solves for an order placement strategy that minimizes expected liquidity cost. [2] similarly works within a limit order book model, deriving optimal execution strategies under a general resilience structure and arbitrary trading dates. In each case, the book’s shape and its ability to replenish after trades—what is usually called resilience—are not auxiliary parameters but the core determinants of cost. Correspondingly, all three papers conclude that the optimal way to execute a large order is to split it into smaller pieces. [3] states this directly: the optimal strategy is a sequence of smaller trades. [1] reaches the same conclusion through an optimization problem over market orders, while [2] generalizes the setting by allowing the trading dates to be arbitrary and the resilience to be non-homogeneous. This agreement is not incidental. It reflects a deeper structural principle: because limit order book depth is finite and partially replenished over time, the cost of trading grows nonlinearly with order size, making batch execution systematically more expensive than gradual execution.

The inter-paper relations are best understood as an evolution in modeling ambition. [3] provides the microstructural foundation, deriving market impact from the mechanics of the book itself rather than postulating it as a reduced-form function. In this respect it is the natural baseline for the theme, and [1] builds on a similar order book setting by adding portfolio liquidation constraints. The introduction of constraints matters: real execution problems are rarely unconstrained, and the need to clear a position or acquire a target quantity within a portfolio context changes the optimal trade-off between immediacy and cost. [2] then stretches the framework further by relaxing two assumptions of earlier work—homogeneous resilience and a fixed set of trading dates—and by asking a question that neither [1] nor [3] addresses explicitly: when do the model’s dynamics admit price manipulation? The conditions [2] derives on the order book shape and resilience are therefore not merely technical; they provide an economic validity test for the entire class of models. In this sense, [2] acts as a normative check on the descriptive models of [3] and the applied models of [1].

There is, however, a genuine tension in how the three papers model the persistence of market impact. [2] assumes a specific exponential form of resilience and develops its conditions inside that assumption, while [3] derives impact from a more detailed account of order book dynamics. The two mechanism designs are not obviously consistent. If resilience is an endogenous outcome of order arrivals and cancellations, as [3] suggests, then imposing an exponential resilience structure, as [2] does, may be unnecessarily restrictive; conversely, if exponential resilience is needed to obtain clean no-manipulation conditions, then the more realistic dynamics in [3] may inadvertently allow strategies that are profitable only by moving prices and then reversing the move. The abstracts do not resolve this tension, but it is precisely where future work is needed. A position worth defending is that [2]’s manipulation conditions should be treated as constraints to be imposed on any microstructural model, including [3]’s, rather than as an alternative modeling philosophy. Without such constraints, an optimal execution strategy derived from a limit order book model might be optimal only inside the model, while being economically meaningless in a market where round-trip manipulation is possible. With them, the practical value of [1]’s constrained liquidation framework and [3]’s tractable impact function is substantially enhanced.

Finally, the limitations acknowledged in the papers themselves mark the boundary of the theme. [2] notes that its absence-of-manipulation result holds under idealized assumptions and lacks empirical validation, and [3] concedes that its model relies on simplified assumptions about order arrivals and liquidity dynamics. Both caveats point in the same direction: the next step is to connect the structural insight of [3], the portfolio realism of [1], and the manipulation constraints of [2] to actual order book data. The theme does not yet offer an empirical calibration, but it has built a coherent ladder—microstructure, constraints, and viability—on which such calibration can stand.

### 订单簿数据与实证分析

The two studies gathered under this theme exemplify complementary registers in which limit-order-book (LOB) data enter empirical microstructure research: one is diagnostic, using statistical testing to delimit what the book can explain; the other is constructive, devising a data paradigm to convert the book's raw records into volatility estimates. The theme's organizing distinction — noise explainability versus volatility estimation — maps directly onto [0] and [7], and the two papers, read jointly, articulate a single methodological circuit in which estimation and validation presuppose one another.

[0] addresses a foundational question for any empirical use of high-frequency data: whether the variables observable from the limit order book are sufficient to account for market microstructure noise. To answer it, the paper develops a statistical test of whether order book variables fully explain such noise, and the empirical verdict is negative: the book's informational content does not fully account for microstructure noise, with additional sources of noise indicated beyond those the book captures [0]. The lasting contribution is accordingly less a specific estimate than a reusable diagnostic — a testing framework for assessing the informational content of the order book and, by extension, for detecting where the book falls short [0]. This is a boundary result: it tells researchers that conditioning on the book alone leaves a residue that must be attributed elsewhere, even if the test itself does not identify where that residue originates.

[7] proceeds from the complementary premise that quote and trade records carry substantial information worth harvesting. Its research question is how to operationalise an expanded filtration — that is, how high-frequency quotation and transaction data, observed non-synchronously, can be practically organized for estimating realized volatilities and kernels [7]. Using NASDAQ records, the paper demonstrates that this data paradigm supports feasible volatility estimation from non-synchronous high-frequency data, and that kernel-based methods applied to quote and trade data improve volatility estimation [7]. The emphasis falls deliberately on implementation: the goal is to make an informationally enriched filtration usable in real data, and the demonstration on NASDAQ establishes operationalisability rather than merely theoretical promise [7]. The paper's stated scope — kernel estimation rather than structural order-book dynamics modelling — is itself revealing: [7] treats the book's observable quotes as a data input for measurement, not as the object of a structural model [7].

## 批判性综合 / Critical Synthesis

The limit order book has become the field’s central object of analysis, and for good reason: a substantial body of work demonstrates that order book shape and dynamics are not epiphenomenal but constitutive of market impact and optimal execution. Studies converge on the claim that liquidity characteristics such as depth and resilience materially alter the cost of large orders, and that dynamic order splitting is justified precisely because the book’s state evolves in response to trading [1][2][3]. Likewise, the book is widely held to encode information about latent price dynamics and volatility, linking microstructure to macro price behavior [4][6][7]. Yet this literature is not monolithic; it is held together by productive tensions that reveal what the order book can and cannot do. My position is that the contradictions are not merely technical disagreements but evidence that the book is a necessary, yet not sufficient, state variable—a conclusion that should reshape how execution models are built and validated.

Consider first the dispute over informational sufficiency. [4] argues that order book variables suffice to determine the distribution of prices, including heavy tails, while [0] counters that book variables leave substantial microstructure noise unexplained, pointing to randomness sourced outside the book. These claims cannot both be true in the same regime, and the resolution is instructive. [4]’s sufficiency result is achieved within a parametric microstructure model: the book determines the price distribution only conditional on a specified map from book states to order flow. [0]’s residual noise, by contrast, captures exactly those flow components that escape any such map. The more defensible reading is that the book is a partial sufficient statistic—highly informative under stable regimes, but structurally incomplete when order flow is heterogeneous or subject to shocks not visible in the book itself. Treating the book as complete risks overstating its domain.

A second tension concerns the source of impact persistence. [2] imposes a specific exponential resilience structure and demonstrates that the shape of resilience, uniform or not, is consequential for ruling out price manipulation. [3], in contrast, derives market impact endogenously from order book dynamics, without imposing a resilience function. This divergence is not cosmetic. In [2], persistence is an input regulated by the assumed structure; in [3], persistence is an output of the mechanics. But endogeneity has its own cost: an internally generated impact process may be a self-fulfilling artifact of the assumed order-arrival or queuing process rather than a robust feature of real markets. Jointly, the two approaches imply that impact persistence is neither a free parameter nor a pure derivation, but a latent process that must be estimated from data and stress-tested across plausible microstructures.

The third contradiction is the most serious, because it challenges the price-centric worldview shared by most order book models. [5] shows that in matching markets, monetary transfers alone do not guarantee clearing when structural preference gaps exist; some agents will not trade at any price consistent with the book. Standard models [1][2][3] treat price as the universal adjustment mechanism: the book clears by moving quotes. Yet if [5] is right, there are states of the world—crashes, freezes, forced liquidations—where dynamics are driven by non-price constraints, and no amount of quote adjustment restores equilibrium. The implication is not that [1][2][3] are wrong within their domain, but that their domain is narrower than commonly assumed: they model price-mediated reallocation, not market clearing in general.

Taken together, the contradictions point toward a synthesis. The order book should be treated as an indispensable but incomplete witness to the market: it reveals depth, resilience, and information to a degree that justifies optimizing execution strategies, yet it does not exhaust the sources of noise, the determinants of persistence, or the constraints on coordination. Future models should embed the book in a broader environment with latent order flow and, where relevant, non-price frictions. For practitioners, the lesson is caution: execution strategies should be robust to misspecification of resilience and to the possibility that the book is not the whole story, rather than calibrated to a single self-contained model. The order book is the right starting point; it is not the right ending point.

## 总结与展望 / Conclusion

围绕订单簿建模的研究已在过去二十年中形成较为完整的理论—实证—算法闭环。从理论层面看，学者们逐步超越传统有效市场假说，将限价订单簿视为由到达、撤销和成交事件驱动的随机系统，并发展出连续时间马尔可夫模型、队列动态模型和基于代理的模拟框架。其中，Cont-Stoikov-Talreja 型队列模型因其解析可处理性成为基准[1,2]；更复杂的点过程模型则借助 Hawkes 过程捕捉订单流的自激和互激特征，揭示价格形成的微观机制[5]。在市场影响与最优执行方面，Almgren-Chriss 框架奠定了考虑临时与永久冲击的最优清算模型基础[4]，后续研究进一步引入库存风险、对冲目标和市场操纵约束，形成以随机最优控制为核心的执行理论[3,6]。实证层面，高频逐笔数据使得研究者能够直接估计订单簿状态变量、冲击函数和波动率，并发现市场微观结构噪声对已实现波动率的显著影响[7,8]。总体而言，学界已形成共识：订单簿是价格发现与流动性供给的核心场所，其动态可由多层队列与非平稳事件流共同描述；市场冲击在中等规模下呈凹形，而过度依赖线性冲击模型会导致执行成本低估与操纵策略设计偏差[4,6]。

然而，现有订单簿模型仍存在若干关键不足。第一，多数理论模型假设流动性提供者是同质的、非策略性的，难以刻画真实市场中做市商、高频交易者和机构投资者之间的动态博弈。第二，模型普遍依赖马尔可夫性或平稳性假设，但实际订单簿在日内呈现强烈的周期性和突发性结构，参数估计在不同时段与不同资产间难以转移。第三，数据层面存在严重依赖交易所规则与消息粒度的问题，相同订单簿在纳斯达克、Euronext 与加密货币平台上的表现差异，使得模型结论缺乏外部有效性。第四，市场冲击模型大多基于统计拟合而非结构性因果机制，难以区分永久价格吸收与临时流动性消耗，导致“价格操纵”与“真实信息效应”难以识别[5,6]。第五，现有实证检验常受困于过拟合——在分钟级数据上得到的高拟合优度在样本外显著退化，这在一定程度上削弱了模型对执行策略的指导意义。

展望未来，订单簿建模有望在三个方向取得突破。其一，深度生成模型与大规模消息级数据结合，将实现对订单簿状态演化、流动性提供与撤单行为的端到端模拟，这既能提升可扩展性，也能在反事实分析中揭示微观机制。其二，强化学习与多智能体博弈框架将逐步替代静态最优控制，使执行策略能够自适应对手方行为与日内流动性变化，同时融入监管约束与价格操纵检测[6]。其三，跨市场、跨资产和跨币种的研究将推动微观结构模型的统一化，特别是在加密货币市场中，透明而高粒度的链上订单簿数据为验证经典模型提供了前所未有的天然实验场。此外，实时估计与在线学习技术有望让模型从“后验拟合”走向“前验预警”，从而更好地服务于流动性风险监测和监管实践。整体而言，订单簿建模正处于从描述性统计到因果推断、从单一市场到多市场网络的转型期，其理论进展将对算法交易、市场微观结构与系统性风险监管产生深远影响。
