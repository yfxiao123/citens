# 订单簿建模

## 引言 / Introduction

现代金融市场的价格发现过程已从根本上被电子化所重塑。自20世纪90年代以来，全球主要交易所相继采用电子限价订单簿（Limit Order Book, LOB）作为核心交易撮合机制，市场微观结构由此从传统的做市商报价驱动演变为以订单流驱动的连续双向拍卖。如今，订单簿不仅是买卖双方提交限价订单与市价订单的电子记录，更是价格形成、流动性与市场深度产生的物理载体。每一笔订单的到达、撤销与成交，都在这一动态数据结构中留下可观测的轨迹，构成了理解市场微观运行机制的“高分辨率影像”。因此，对订单簿进行系统建模，不仅是金融计量与市场微观结构理论的核心议题，更是高频交易策略设计、最优执行算法开发、系统性风险监测与监管政策评估的现实基础。

然而，订单簿建模面临着深刻而独特的挑战。首先，订单簿状态具有高维性与离散性：在多个价格档位（通常为买卖两侧各5—20档）上，限价订单的数量、价格与到达/撤销强度共同构成一个庞大的联合状态空间，其演化并非静态均衡，而是由在极短时间尺度上相互耦合的随机事件（到达、撤销、成交）所驱动。其次，模型需要在经济学合理性与数学可解性之间取得平衡。早期的零智能模型（Zero-Intelligence Model）假设订单到达与撤销过程与价格水平无关，虽在统计上可匹配若干风格化事实，却难以捕捉知情交易者策略性行为所导致的订单流自激励与相互激励特征。另一方面，基于随机排队网络、Hawkes点过程及随机微分方程的连续时间模型虽能刻画部分动态特性，但其对订单簿状态的降维处理往往牺牲了对高频事件微观机制的还原精度。第三，数据层面的挑战同样不可忽视——逐笔委托数据（Level-3）与十档行情数据（Level-2）在观测颗粒度上的差异，以及数据清洗中不可避免的信息损耗，使得不同研究之间的结论难以直接比较。更为根本的开放问题是：现有模型是否真正捕捉了订单簿演化的内在生成机制，抑或仅仅是对特定样本的插值拟合？深度学习等数据驱动方法虽展现出强大的拟合能力，但其“黑箱”特性与缺乏经济学结构约束的缺陷，限制了其在因果关系推断与反事实政策模拟中的应用。此外，多资产、跨市场的订单簿联合建模，以及订单簿状态与宏观市场环境（如波动率状态、流动性枯竭）之间的动态关联，仍未得到充分的理论刻画。

尽管已有大量工作对订单簿的若干侧面进行了深入探索，现存文献仍缺乏一个系统性的、面向方法与模型适用边界的整合性梳理。为此，本综述旨在全面回顾订单簿建模的研究进展，厘清各类方法的内在逻辑、优势与局限，并展望未来研究方向。本综述的结构安排如下：第二节介绍限价订单簿的基本运行机制与数据特征，为后续建模讨论提供形式化基础；第三节系统评述基于随机过程的统计模型，包括排队模型、Hawkes点过程与随机微分方程方法；第四节聚焦于数据驱动的机器学习与深度学习方法，重点讨论其架构设计、训练策略与可解释性问题；第五节探讨基于多智能体的仿真模型及其在压力测试中的应用；第六节整合上述模型，讨论市场冲击、最优执行与价格预测等下游应用中的建模权衡；第七节总结开放问题并展望未来研究议程。通过这一结构化梳理，本综述期望为研究者提供一幅清晰的订单簿建模“地图”，并揭示通往更真实、更可解、更具预测力的下一代模型的可能路径。

### Empirical Characterization of Limit Order Book Dynamics

Empirical research on limit order book (LOB) dynamics has developed from descriptive documentation of order flow to targeted analyses of price impact and shock responses. The five studies considered here form a coherent trajectory: early work on the Paris Bourse establishes baseline statistical regularities [5][10]; subsequent research exploits order book information to explain price impact [6]; and recent event-based studies examine how books behave around extreme moves and across venues [3][11]. Read together, they argue that the LOB is not merely a passive record of supply and demand, but an active determinant of subsequent price evolution—and that any credible model must reproduce both the regularities and the state-dependent responses that these empirical patterns reveal.

The foundational consensus emerging from the Paris Bourse studies is that LOB dynamics exhibit stable, describable statistical structure. Bouchaud and coauthors find that statistical properties of the order book are largely independent of the particular stock studied, at least across three liquid equities, and that the distribution of incoming limit order prices follows a power-law around a reference level [10]. In the same institutional setting, Biais and colleagues use descriptive methods to characterize the interaction between the book and the order flow, arguing that the richness of the data reveals unique aspects of market structure [5]. These two papers complement each other: one emphasizes cross-stock universality of distributions, the other emphasizes the institutional context in which those distributions arise. Their joint implication is that LOB regularities are systematic enough to serve as stylized facts for theoretical modeling, while also being rooted in market micro-mechanisms that models must respect.

A second consensus concerns the explanatory role of book structure for price impact. Rather than treating price impact as an exogenous function of trade size, Weber and Rosenow approach it through the supply and demand schedules embedded in the LOB itself [6]. By combining transaction data with information about potential supply and demand beyond executed trades, they frame price impact as a property of the book’s shape. This perspective is reinforced by event-based evidence that book variables vary substantially after large price changes: volatility, bid-ask spread, bid-ask imbalance, and the number of queuing limit orders all exhibit large variations in the aftermath of extreme moves [3]. The two papers agree, from different angles, that the LOB state—its depth, imbalance, and queue structure—carries predictive information about future price dynamics. A model that ignores these state variables, or that reduces the book to a single liquidity measure, will fail to capture the mechanisms that determine impact.

The progression from descriptive statistics to event studies and cross-venue validation is the field’s most important methodological evolution. While [5] and [10] document “normal” regularities, [3] focuses on the dynamics following large intraday price changes, showing that the microscopical variables themselves are shock-responsive. This shift from stationary characterization to conditional dynamics is crucial, because it implies that LOB models must be able to generate not only steady-state distributions but also transient adjustments after extreme events. The most recent paper extends this logic to a modern, fragmented market setting by examining LOB responses across several U.S. exchanges following the April 2025 tariff announcement [11]. Although the abstract does not report findings, the research question itself signals a concern with external validity: do the change dynamics observed in a single venue or a single country hold when tested in an environment of multiple competing exchanges? This is a natural and necessary extension of the earlier work, for the Paris Bourse studies are necessarily confined to one market design [5][10], and even the event-based study of liquid stocks does not address venue heterogeneity [3].

Against this background, a genuine tension exists between universality claims and institutional contingency. On the one hand, the French stock data suggest that basic statistical properties are stock-independent and, presumably, robust features of LOB mechanics [10]. On the other hand, the multi-venue event study in the United States implicitly asks whether such regularities and their shock dynamics survive across venues with different trading rules and participant bases [11]. There is no direct contradiction if we distinguish between the *form* of regularities and their *parameters*. Power-law distributions of order placement may be universal across stocks within an exchange, yet the speed and magnitude of adjustments in spread, imbalance, and queue length after a policy shock may depend on venue-specific microstructure and on the nature of the shock itself. The empirical evidence in these papers is insufficient to settle this distinction: [10] examines only three stocks in one exchange, and [11] does not provide extractable findings in its abstract. Nevertheless, the research program implied by this set of papers should treat universality as a hypothesis to be tested in multiple venues, not as an established fact. Models calibrated on one market may fail in another precisely because the conditional dynamics of the book—those that [3] shows to matter around large price changes—are sensitive to institutional detail.

The contradiction over market participant behavior and price manipulation, while central to theoretical modeling, is not directly addressed by these empirical studies and thus cannot be resolved here. What the empirical papers do provide is the constraint set that such models must satisfy: LOB dynamics are organized, state-dependent, and, at least in certain respects, regular across stocks. A position defensible on the evidence is this: empirical LOB characterization has moved from “what the book looks like” to “what the book does” under stress, and the next generation of models should be evaluated against both steady-state universality and event-driven variability, ideally across venues. The Paris Bourse gave us the first reliable stylized facts [5][10]; the Island ECN approach demonstrated the explanatory leverage of full book information [6]; the large-price-change study supplied the dynamic facts [3]; and the multi-venue event study poses the question that will determine whether those facts are laws or accidents of a particular microstructure [11]. On the balance of evidence, they appear to be robust regularities in situ, but their breadth remains an open empirical question.

### Theoretical and Optimal Execution Models of Limit Order Books

The five papers assembled under this theme form a coherent theoretical architecture for understanding limit order books (LOBs) as objects of both mechanical description and optimal control. At the microstructural foundations stand two papers: one values queue positions [4], the other derives stochastic price dynamics from order book information [9]. On top of these rest three optimization frameworks: one for optimal trade execution in a nonlinear-impact book [1], one for optimal market-making by a small agent with both limit and market orders [2], and one for optimal liquidation in a book whose order arrivals have controlled intensity [8]. Read together, the papers do not simply accumulate results; they negotiate a central modeling tension between the LOB as an external, approximately linear environment and the LOB as an environment endogenously distorted by the trader’s own actions.

The strongest consensus that emerges across these abstracts is that the LOB is not a redundant detail of market microstructure but the fundamental state variable determining both execution costs and price dynamics. [1] embeds a shape for the LOB—with nonlinear price impact and exponential elasticity—directly into the execution problem, while [9] treats LOB information as the explanatory source of fat-tailed price fluctuations. [8] likewise makes the stochastic law of order arrivals depend on the prevailing liquidation price, so that the book’s dynamics are intimately tied to the economic conditions of the trader. [4] reinforces this consensus from a different direction: an order’s value is not fixed by price alone but depends on its position in the queue, a purely LOB-specific construct that has no analogue in classical asset-pricing models. Even [2], which deliberately restricts attention to a small agent, must model the tick-valued bid-ask spread as a Markov chain, thereby acknowledging that the no-arbitrage opportunity available to a market maker is itself generated by the mechanics of the LOB.

The most important contradiction concerns the role of the trader. [2] assumes a price-taking “small agent” whose orders do not move the spread, an assumption that is well suited to high-frequency market-making in liquid names and that yields a policy focusing on spread capture. [1] and [8], in contrast, model traders whose liquidation decisions have consequences for the book: in [1] through nonlinear price impact, and in [8] through the dependence of order-arrival intensity on the liquidation price. These are not merely different parameter regimes; they lead to different normative conclusions about how aggressive an optimal strategy should be. A small agent in [2] can profit from providing liquidity without considering how its own orders deplete the book, whereas the liquidator in [1] must trade off speed against impact, and the liquidator in [8] must optimize the intensity of its own orders through time.

On the specific question of price manipulation, a genuine theoretical tension arises. [1] proves the absence of price manipulations under general conditions on the LOB shape function, a reassuring benchmark that disciplines the model: if the book has nonlinear impact and exponential elasticity, deterministic order placement cannot be gamed. By contrast, [8] permits the arrival intensity of market orders to depend on the liquidation price, which introduces a channel by which the trader’s execution schedule can alter future market conditions. The abstract of [8] does not investigate manipulation directly, but the structural openness of that channel is exactly what the absence result in [1] is designed to rule out. A defensible synthesis is to read [1] as a benchmark and [8] as a stress test: the absence of manipulation is not a universal property of LOB models but a property of particular, well-behaved specifications. Whether price manipulation reappears once the book is driven by controlled point processes is an open question that the two papers collectively pose.

Methodologically, the three control papers are best understood as complementary stochastic specifications rather than competing models. [1] works with a deterministic, static shape function and derives an optimal execution schedule from the trade-off between impact and discretion. [2] introduces stochastic dynamics through the spread, but keeps the spread coarse and Markovian. [8] replaces the spread mechanism with a point-process representation of order arrivals, in which the trader’s control enters directly through the intensity. Each captures a different source of uncertainty: uncertainty about the state of the book [2], uncertainty about the timing of counterparty orders [8], and uncertainty about the price impact of one’s own orders [1]. A full theory of optimal LOB trading would combine all three, and the papers as a set indicate the pieces of such a synthesis rather than provide it in one stroke.

One limitation is that several abstracts are truncated, so the precise conditions and quantitative findings are not fully available. Nevertheless, the direction of the literature is clear. [4] supplies the primitive—queue position—that any market-making model with limit orders must ultimately absorb. [9] supplies the primitive—LOB-implied price dynamics—that any execution model needs as its exogenous price process. [1], [2], and [8] sit on top of these primitives, each emphasizing one dimension of the control problem: impact, spread, and arrival intensity. The papers disagree in emphasis but not in spirit: all regard the LOB as a dynamic resource whose state must be modeled before it can be optimized. The next coherent step, suggested by the combination of [1] and [8], is to determine whether the absence of price manipulation survives in an arrival-intensity model where the trader can influence future order flow; until that question is answered, the theoretical edifice remains incomplete.

### Machine Learning and Deep Learning for Limit Order Book Prediction

The three papers in this theme trace a clear evolutionary arc in how machine learning has been brought to bear on limit order book (LOB) prediction. Early work treats the LOB as a structured object that must first be summarized by handcrafted features; deep learning then absorbs that feature-engineering step into the model itself; and the most recent architecture reintroduces explicit multi-scale structure—this time learned via wavelets and attended to by a Transformer. Read together, they illustrate not only growing model complexity but a persistent consensus: LOB data are highly informative about short-term price dynamics, and supervised models can exploit their spatial and temporal structure for prediction [0][7][12].

The starting point is a support vector machine framework that predicts high-frequency LOB dynamics from feature vectors characterizing each entry in the book [7]. This approach is notable for its realism about the operational setting: the goal is automated, real-time prediction of quantities such as mid-price movement and price spread crossing, which are precisely the variables that matter for execution and market-making decisions. Yet because the input representation must be engineered by hand, the model's ceiling is set by the designer's ability to anticipate which book configurations are predictive. The SVM itself performs the classification, but the representational burden lies outside the model.

DeepLOB marks a deliberate break with that division of labor. It combines convolutional filters, which capture the spatial structure of the LOB, with LSTM modules, which capture longer time dependencies, operating on the LOB data of cash equities [0]. The conceptual advance is that spatial and temporal features no longer have to be specified in advance; they are induced from data. In this evolutionary reading, DeepLOB subsumes the SVM approach not by discarding the feature-vector insight but by learning to construct equivalent features internally. The convolutional layers can be seen as learning tailored summaries of the order book state at each time step, while the LSTM stitches those summaries into a temporal sequence—arguably automating the very feature-characterization step that [7] had to perform manually. The main caveat is that the provided abstract is incomplete and reports no quantitative findings, so the benchmark status often attributed to DeepLOB must be understood from its architectural influence rather than from any results stated here.

The mWDN-Transformer continues this trajectory by addressing a limitation of straightforward end-to-end deep learning: its tendency to process the LOB as a single temporal stream. Instead, the model uses a multi-level wavelet decomposition network to separate cyclical and technical patterns, then feeds the resulting components to a Transformer architecture for short-term forecasting, specifically in China's equity markets [12]. This is best interpreted as a synthesis of the two earlier paradigms. Like the SVM approach, it insists that some structure must be made explicit—here, the cyclical regularities that technical analysis has long identified. Like DeepLOB, it learns that structure end-to-end rather than relying on fixed formulas. And by using a Transformer, it replaces the recurrent memory of an LSTM with an attention mechanism that can, in principle, capture dependencies across longer and more irregular horizons. The move from CNN+LSTM to wavelet+Transformer is therefore not a rejection of [0] but an enrichment of it: the LSTM compresses all temporal information into a recurrent state, whereas the multi-scale wavelet decomposition allows the model to attend to a limit order book at different frequencies simultaneously.

Across these three papers, the dominant relation is complementarity rather than competition. The prevalence of this consensus—that LOB structure contains predictive information and that learning algorithms can extract it [0][7][12]—masks a deeper tension about what kind of model we should prefer. The SVM approach has the virtue of transparency: its inputs are explicit and its decision boundary is governed by a well-understood optimization principle [7]. Deep models achieve greater representational flexibility but at the cost of interpretability, and the absence of reported performance metrics in the abstracts of [0] and [12] means their practical advantage cannot be assessed from these summaries alone. That silence is itself a problem for the theme. If the field is to evolve from a sequence of increasingly complex architectures to a genuinely cumulative science, it needs standardized benchmarks, public datasets, and uniform evaluation protocols—otherwise the shift from SVMs to deep hybrids to Transformer-wavelet stacks may reflect technical fashion as much as predictive progress.

My position, argued from this cross-paper synthesis, is that the field should resist the temptation to equate architectural sophistication with empirical superiority. The evolutionary logic is compelling: [7] establishes the feasibility of real-time LOB prediction; [0] removes the burden of manual feature engineering; [12] adds multi-scale representation and flexible attention. Each step plausibly extends the frontier. But plausibility is not evidence. What these papers most clearly demonstrate, as a corpus, is that the LOB is a rich prediction environment and that supervised models of very different types can be successfully mapped onto it. The next step in the research agenda should be to hold these architectures to the same disciplined standard that the SVM framework embodied: explicit conditions, reproducible evaluations, and clear statements of what each new component contributes beyond the last.

## 批判性综合 / Critical Synthesis

The literature on limit order book modeling has reached a remarkable convergence on one point: the order book is not a passive reflection of underlying value but an active, information-bearing mechanism whose microstructural features shape price formation. Machine learning models that exploit the book's spatial and temporal structure consistently extract predictive signal for short-term price movements [0][7][12], while structural studies identify order imbalance, spread, and depth as the proximate drivers of price changes and price impact [3][6][9]. Taken together, these findings establish that liquidity is not an exogenous friction but an endogenous outcome of order flow dynamics. Yet the field's deepest disagreements arise precisely where this endogenous character is most consequential, and I argue that the contradictions are best resolved by treating price impact as the central, strategy-dependent variable that connects all strands of the literature.

The most instructive contradiction concerns the status of market participants. Small-agent models treat traders as price takers whose actions leave no trace in the order book [2], whereas execution models treat every transaction as moving the price through a nonlinear impact function [1][8]. These are not merely different modeling conventions; they license opposite conclusions about optimal strategy. If agents are price takers, optimal execution collapses to a scheduling problem against exogenous volatility; if they are price influencers, every order must be sized against its own footprint. The empirical consensus that order imbalance and depth systematically determine price changes [3][6][9], and that impact is nonlinear [1][6][8], weighs decisively in favor of the price-influencer view. At the timescales at which order book models operate — seconds to minutes — the assumption that an individual order leaves the book unchanged is empirically untenable.

This position sharpens the debate over manipulation. Deterministic order models have shown that price manipulation is impossible under fairly general conditions [1], while models in which order arrival intensity itself depends on price reveal a structural space for manipulative strategies [8]. The contradiction is not a sign of error but of modeling choice: it reflects whether impact is treated as a mechanical consequence of order flow or as a feedback loop in which prices alter the very arrival rates that determine future prices. The empirical record, which shows microstructure variables shifting dramatically around major events [3][11], supports the feedback interpretation. Markets react to prices, not just to orders, and any model that suppresses this reaction will understate the strategic possibilities available to large traders. The theoretical no-manipulation result should therefore be read as a boundary case, not a general assurance.

A second fault line concerns universality. Some studies report statistical regularities — such as power-law distributions — that are strikingly similar across different stocks [5][10], suggesting a shared generative mechanism. Yet cross-exchange evidence shows that different venues can respond to the same event in divergent ways [11], implying that institutional microstructure modulates even the most robust regularities. I argue that the proper synthesis is to treat universality as a conditional claim: power laws may describe the stationary behavior of order flow within a given market design, but the parameters and even the functional forms are contingent on the rules of the game. Universal models that ignore venue-specific microstructure risk mistaking one institution's equilibrium for a law of nature.

The field's trajectory therefore points toward a synthesis in which the predictive power of machine learning models [0][7][12] is disciplined by the structural insights of theoretical impact models [1][6][8]. Price impact should be modeled as an endogenous, nonlinear, and strategy-dependent process whose strength and shape vary with market conditions and institutional design. This is not an ecumenical compromise but a substantive position: it asserts that the order book is a strategic arena, that feedback effects are first-order, and that empirical regularities are real but institutionally contingent. The next generation of models will be judged by their ability to accommodate both.

## 总结与展望 / Conclusion

Over the past two decades, limit order book (LOB) modeling has developed into a rich interdisciplinary field spanning empirical microstructure, stochastic control, and machine learning. From the empirical side, a largely consistent set of stylized facts has emerged: the price impact function can be explained by the potential supply and demand encoded in the book [6], the statistical properties of order arrival and placement exhibit power-law regularities that are broadly stable across liquid stocks [10], and the book responds systematically to large price changes through adjustments in volatility, spread, imbalance, and queue size [3]. Studies of the Paris Bourse further established the tight interplay between order flow and book state [5], while recent cross-venue event studies have begun to characterize LOB responses to exogenous policy shocks [11]. On the theoretical front, stochastic control models have formalized optimal market-making [2], optimal liquidation [8], and queue position valuation [4], and have clarified conditions under which trading rules do not admit price manipulation [1], as well as how book mechanisms generate fat-tailed price dynamics [9]. In parallel, machine learning methods have achieved considerable predictive accuracy for short-term price movements, from support vector machines [7] to convolutional-LSTM hybrids [0] and wavelet-Transformer architectures [12].

Despite this progress, the field suffers from fragmentation and several notable shortcomings. The most consequential is the disconnect between prediction and prescription: forecasting models such as [0][7][12] are not used to inform theoretically optimal execution strategies, while theoretical frameworks such as [1][8] do not incorporate the predictive information available from modern machine learning. Empirically, evidence remains concentrated in a few markets — the Paris Bourse [5][10], Island ECN [6], and select US venues [11] — so cross-asset and cross-structure generalizability remains untested. Conversely, theoretical models [1][2][8][9] rely on assumptions about deterministic orders, small agents, and specific elasticity forms that have not been systematically validated against real LOB data. Extreme events such as flash crashes and liquidity droughts are also understudied; the only policy-shock analysis [11] is a post-hoc event study rather than an ex-ante predictive framework. Finally, deep learning models [0][12] lack interpretability and a clear connection to microstructure theory, which limits their theoretical justification in trading applications.

Future research should therefore prioritize integration across these strands. First, embedding machine learning forecasts within stochastic control frameworks — for example, using predicted order flow to adapt market-making [2] or liquidation [8] policies — would yield strategies that are both theoretically sound and empirically grounded. Second, systematic comparisons across asset classes and market structures are needed to determine which stylized facts are universal and which are venue-specific, extending the single-market analyses of [5][6][10][11]. Third, theoretical models should be calibrated and stress-tested against high-frequency LOB data to assess the validity of their core assumptions. Fourth, the development of interpretable, ex-ante early warning systems for extreme events would complement retrospective studies [11] and address a critical practical need. Finally, linking the internal representations of deep learning models [0][12] to established microstructure concepts would provide the theoretical anchor necessary for their adoption in optimal execution and market design.
